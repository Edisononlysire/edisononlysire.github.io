Research trajectory

Graduate research began with a specific question: should cells with similar expression profiles be understood in the same way when they occupy different tissue neighborhoods? In OmniCell, baseline construction, data analysis, and evaluation examined what spatial information contributed to cellular and tissue-level tasks. Work on GOformer involved designing readouts and comparisons for cell, gene, and Gene Ontology representations, separating the quality of pretrained features from gains due to downstream optimization. Together, these studies brought a more fundamental question into focus: how can a model retain both molecular identity and spatial context? For SPARSE, I developed the overall framework, adapted multi-scale ideas from computer vision to spatial-neighborhood modeling, and carried out the downstream evaluations.

Describing a state then raised the question of how an intervention might change it. In DynPerturb, data analysis, model evaluation, and ablations examined the effects of perturbation timing, intensity, and location on cell states and regulatory relationships. Interpreting those results made the need for checkable mechanistic evidence more apparent. The principal contribution to BiOmics was its overall research framework, which connects knowledge relations, latent representations, and specialist tools in a traceable reasoning process. Methodology and analysis for OncoEureka brought together competing oncology hypotheses, prespecified tests, and supporting and contrary findings from distinct data sources, turning preliminary observations into executable plans for the next study.

As the work extended to agents acting over many steps, evidence also had to remain current as a task unfolded. Contributions to EvoState included its research framing and state mechanisms, separating current execution records, past events, and active requirements so new observations do not displace critical constraints. The LexStance concept proposed at Peking University Law School explores a compact, computable record of positions scattered across multi-party dialogue, reducing the need to repeatedly reinterpret the transcript. In cybersecurity defense, AEP-ECRG extends this question to changing source trust and active evidence gathering. A source with a reliable history may later be compromised; the agent must weigh the value of investigation against response delay and the cost of a mistaken containment action before deciding whether the evidence justifies intervention.

Across biological systems and autonomous agents, the recurring question is how a judgment acquires its grounds and remains open to testing as new observations arrive. Future work aims to bring state representation, intervention modeling, mechanistic validation, and task memory into a continuous process. Candidate actions would be presented with their sources, conditions, and unresolved questions, leaving consequential judgments open to review and revision.

BGI Beijing Innovation Center · AI Algorithm R&D

OmniCell

Preprint · co-first author

A foundation model for single-cell and spatial transcriptomics

OmniCell asks one model to learn both expression relationships within a cell and spatial dependencies between cells. It soft-ranks continuous expression values and serializes a target cell with its spatial neighbors; a gene-aware mixture-of-experts module encodes expression, spatially informed attention captures neighborhood structure, and a symmetric bilinear output couples gene and cell representations. This shared representation supports cell-identity, co-expression, and tissue-structure tasks, with cross-task evaluation and ablations testing what each component adds.

Built baseline models and a unified evaluation workflow across single-cell and spatial tasks, aligning controls, splits, and metrics. Conducted data analysis and cross-task model assessment to test whether spatial context improves tissue-level interpretation while preserving discriminative cell and gene information. Contributed to model discussions and the organization of manuscript results. Read the preprint ↗

BGI Beijing Innovation Center · AI Algorithm R&D

GOformer

Preparing for submission

A single-cell foundation model incorporating Gene Ontology priors

GOformer addresses two limits of transcriptomic foundation models: prohibitively long gene sequences and weak structural guidance when learning only from expression correlations. A gene–GO bipartite graph acts as an information bottleneck: a graph encoder gathers gene signals at functional nodes, a Transformer models global relations over the shorter GO sequence, and a reverse graph decoder returns that context to gene representations. Masked-expression reconstruction and momentum contrastive learning constrain gene-level prediction and cell-level embeddings, respectively, allowing structured priors, efficiency, and transfer to be tested together.

Designed downstream readouts and comparisons across cell, gene, and GO representations, separating linear probes on frozen features from task-specific fine-tuning. Compared aggregation and attention-fusion choices, planned ablations, and organized training protocols, task settings, and interpretation of results. The manuscript is being prepared for submission.

Peking University Law School

LexStance

Concept stage

Stance encoding for multi-party legal dialogue

In a legal scenario with several participants, support, objection, or reservations toward a claim can be scattered across many turns of natural-language dialogue. If an agent retains only the transcript, later steps must reread it and infer each person's position again. LexStance explores compressing the exchange into a machine-readable state record of the speaker, the claim, and the current stance, making positions and their changes easier to track.

Proposed a structured speaker–claim–stance record for multi-party dialogue and identified ambiguous positions, stance changes, and conflicting statements as cases the representation must handle. The planned evaluation compares transcript retention with structured state maintenance for stance accuracy and context-token use. The encoding and evaluation are still being designed.

Papers & ongoing projects

State representation · sole first author

SPARSE

ICLR 2027 · under review

Semantic-Preserving Adapter for Robust Spatial Embeddings

SPARSE separates what a pretrained model knows about cell identity from how those cells are organized in space: the transcriptomic backbone stays frozen while a lightweight spatial adapter is trained. Within a fixed physical neighborhood around each cell, feature-guided token merging changes representation granularity, propagates coordinates, adds spatial rotary encoding, and returns coarse-to-fine context through cross-scale attention and top-down fusion. Scale here refers to token resolution, not an expanding radius. Masked-expression reconstruction constrains the adapter to retain molecular semantics while learning spatial structure.

Developed the overall research framework and adapted multi-scale ideas from computer vision to spatial neighborhoods, including the expression-reconstruction constraint for semantic preservation. Conducted downstream experiments on spatial-domain identification, cell-type separation, label transfer, and neighborhood composition; organized baselines and ablations, tested transfer across held-out regions, developmental stages, and transcriptomic backbones, and prepared the figures and manuscript argument.

Dynamic projection · co-author

DynPerturb

Nature Methods · in second-round revision · 2025 IF 28.3

Dynamic Perturbation Modeling for Spatiotemporal Single-Cell Systems

DynPerturb treats an intervention as an event with a time, intensity, and location rather than comparing endpoints alone. It arranges regulatory or spatial relations into chronological event graphs, updating node states with temporal encoding, neighborhood attention, and time-stamped memory. Link prediction models relationship rewiring, while auxiliary node classification retains cell or regional information. Responses under alternative interventions are read from latent-embedding and network changes, yielding testable hypotheses about response windows and feedback rather than direct proof of causal effects.

As documented in the contribution statement, conducted data analysis and model evaluation, examining chronological link prediction, node classification, and stability across intervention settings. Performed ablations, organized comparisons, and contributed to manuscript preparation and its second-round revision, with particular attention to what temporal, spatial, and memory components each explain.

Mechanistic reasoning · co-first author

BiOmics

Nature Biotechnology · planned submission · 2025 IF 44.5

A Foundational Bio-Reasoning Agent for Traceable and Mechanistic Multi-omics Discovery

BiOmics starts from a methodological gap: statistical models can propose associations without showing their basis, while knowledge retrieval can recover facts without predicting missing links. It therefore maintains explicit reasoning and latent representation spaces in parallel. A knowledge graph supplies inspectable entity–relation paths, the BRICK toolchain connects omics data to retrieval, ranking, analysis, and graph representation learning, and an Agent plans their use for each question. Known relations constrain and audit explanations; joint embeddings propose candidate associations, completing a retrieve–reason–predict loop that can be checked.

As co-first author, shaped the research idea and overall framework, proposing the combination of explicit knowledge paths and latent representations. Planned the roles, data flow, and evidence-checking steps of BiOmics-KG, BRICK, and the Agent; designed the workflow from question parsing to candidate prediction; and discussed controls and manuscript framing for variant, cell-annotation, and drug-repurposing tasks. The draft remains under joint revision.

Evidence-driven oncology · co-first author

OncoEureka

The Lancet Summit · abstract submitted

An evidence-driven AI agent for oncology hypothesis generation and iterative research design

OncoEureka turns an oncology signal into a research procedure that can be challenged. The agent first defines the question and data boundaries, proposes competing mechanistic hypotheses, and fixes directional predictions, an analysis plan, and validation routes before inspecting candidate-specific results. Discovery, held-out, and external evidence are then compared, including support, contrary findings, and unresolved gaps. Examples in acute myeloid leukemia drug sensitivity and cervical-cancer treatment response show how an initial association can lead to the next longitudinal, single-cell, spatial, or functional study.

As co-first author, contributed to methodology and analysis, especially the separation of candidate generation, prespecified testing, and follow-up validation. Organized how evidence from different sources should be compared, what would count against a hypothesis, and how preliminary associations become research questions with directional predictions.

Persistent decisions · co-first author

EvoState

ICLR 2027 · under review

Multi-Operation Context-State Transitions for Long-Horizon LLM Agents

EvoState treats context compression in long-horizon agents as state maintenance: a new observation may add information or invalidate an old fact. Current Trace stores exact execution evidence, Event Memory retains historical outcomes, and Task State records valid facts and unresolved requirements. A multi-operation editor revises these records as evidence arrives; a dependency-aware assembler selects what the next action needs. Exact identifiers, paths, and unresolved dependencies can exceed a soft token target so compression does not discard an action prerequisite.

Contributed to the research question, the three-part state design, and their retention rules, specifying how exact references, historical outcomes, current facts, and open requirements should be updated. Helped design multi-operation editing and dependency-aware assembly, including stale-fact invalidation and the trade-off between next-action dependencies and a soft token budget, and contributed to evaluation and ablation design.

Cybersecurity defense · ongoing

AEP-ECRG

Exploratory

AEP-ECRG addresses two uncertainties in cybersecurity defense: alerts and telemetry may not distinguish an attack from legitimate change, while a previously trusted log, identity, or detection source may be compromised and still issue a formally valid but false claim. The framework tracks source reputation separately from the trustworthiness of each claim, updating both as supporting or conflicting evidence arrives. Its Active Evidence Policy (AEP) weighs investigation cost, response delay, and the business impact of wrongful isolation when choosing whether to observe, verify a source, or respond. The Evidence-Constrained Runtime Governor (ECRG) checks whether evidence is relevant to the target and proposed action before isolation, removal, or another consequential step; a valid signature or good past reputation alone is not treated as sufficient grounds.

Developed a research framework that combines active evidence gathering with evidence-constrained governance, linking investigative choices to the evidentiary threshold for consequential defensive action. Designed paired scenarios involving ambiguous alerts, compromised trusted sources, and legitimate system changes to examine how public evidence and source trust shape investigation budgets and security utility. The evaluation plan uses source verification and canary probes, then compares decisions when a probe yields useful information, yields none, or is not attempted. These comparisons test whether the system can separate suspicion of a source from evidence sufficient to justify a restrictive measure. The engineering prototype and evaluation protocol are being refined.
